# Reflective Reading 3: Lim et al.: The Anatomy of Prototypes

When I started reading the article, I thought that I would find a list about prototypes and how/when to use them. It instead showed me and idea that helped me to see my own proyects in a different way: a prototype is not an incompleted version of a project, it's a filter. Something that, since is "incomplete" , let us see a single quality of an idea without the rest distracting us.

The thing I found the most interesting of the text was that prototypes are manifestations of ideas. The article talks about how the act of having a thought and transform it into a material form is not only one step of a process, but a part of the creation itself. They quote Schön when he says that we have to externalize our ideas so that "the world can speak back to us". Something similar happened to me when I was working on my FPS prototype in Unity: I didn't fully understand my own idea until I saw it working, even if it was in a clumsy way. The prototype wasn't showing me what I already knew, it was helping me discover what I didn't know yet.

I also kept thinking about the two case studies. The first one compares a 2D blueprint of a house with a 3D virtual model of the same house. Even though they represent exactly the same idea, the people who used the blueprint talked about the layout and the relationships between spaces, and even proposed how to remodel the house. The people who used the 3D model talked about how the house felt, about colors and textures, and commented that it was small. Even when drawing the floor plan from memory, the 3D group couldn't place yhe rooms correctly. This made me understand that a prototype is not neutral: the way you manifest an idea changes what people perceive from it, and also what you perceive yourself.

The second case felt even closer to me, because it compares three prototypes of a mobile phone: one made of paper, one on a computer screen, and onefully functional. All of them evaluated the same thing, the usuability of text messaging, but only twenty percent of the findings were shared among all three. The paper prototype confused users because they couldn't press the buttons, and the screen one leds them to try to click directly on the image of the phone. This reminded me of a problem I had with my 3D snowman on my third prototype, which kept getting under the player. At first I thought it was a problem with my code, but it was actually a problem with the prototype: I wasn't testing the interaction I was interessed on, but something else that had nothing to do with my idea. I even commented many parts of the snowman script and indirectly created prototypes just in case the new code I was writting to modify or change its actions didn't work (which many times didn't worked).

The article proposes that a prototype has two sides: the filters (which aspects of the idea we choose to show, like appearence, data, interactivity) and the manifestation (how we show it, meaning the material, resolution and scope). And what I value most about this reading is that it doesn't present this as a recipe, but as a way of thinking. A "good" prototype doesn't exist in the abstract, it can only be understood in relation to what you want to explore at that moment.
